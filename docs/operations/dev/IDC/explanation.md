# BANKON PYTHAI: Multi-Chain Existence and Self-Sustaining Arbitrage

## Introduction

This document addresses two questions that operators, integrators, and treasury managers need to understand before deploying or interacting with BANKON PYTHAI across multiple blockchains. The first question is conceptual: how can a single token with a fixed total supply exist simultaneously on Ethereum, Arbitrum, Optimism, Base, Polygon, and Algorand, particularly in the case where liquidity is concentrated on a single chain and the others host only the token contract? The second question is operational: how does the protocol structure an arbitrage engine that maintains price parity across the mesh while remaining self-funded from the spreads it captures, rather than draining the treasury to subsidize convergence?

Both questions reduce to the same underlying property of the design, which is that BANKON PYTHAI separates the question of where the token exists from the question of where it is liquid, and uses economic incentive rather than protocol fiat to keep prices aligned across the chains where both conditions hold. The mechanism is mathematically self-sustaining within bounds set by transaction costs, and the document below works through those bounds in detail and shows how the protocol captures the residual value for the treasury rather than dissipating it.

## Part One: The Existence Model

### What It Means for a Token to Exist on a Chain

A token exists on a blockchain when there is a deployed smart contract on that chain capable of holding balances, executing transfers, and reporting state through the standard ERC20 interface. Existence in this sense is independent of trading activity, liquidity provision, or price discovery. A user on Optimism can hold a balance of BANKON PYTHAI, transfer it to another Optimism address, sign a permit allowing a contract to spend it, and call any standard ERC20 function, all without any AMM pool, any market maker, or any price feed existing on Optimism. The token is real on that chain because the contract is deployed and the user's balance is recorded in its storage.

This is distinct from a wrapped token or a synthetic. A wrapped representation, such as the legacy USDC.e on bridged L2 deployments, depends on a custodian holding the underlying asset on the home chain and issuing IOUs on the destination. A synthetic, such as a perpetual contract or a contract-for-difference, depends on a collateral pool and an oracle to mark a derivative position. BANKON PYTHAI is neither. On every chain where the contract is deployed, the token holders' balances are first-class entries in that chain's state, not claims against external collateral, and the supply on that chain is reduced or increased only through legitimate LayerZero OFT operations that maintain the global invariant.

### The Burn-and-Mint Supply Invariant

The mechanism that makes multi-chain existence possible without inflation is burn-and-mint. The canonical mint of 111,111.111111111111111111 PYTHAI occurs exactly once, in the constructor of the contract deployed on the chosen canonical chain — for BANKON this is Ethereum mainnet — and the entire global supply enters circulation at that single moment. Every other chain's contract is deployed with zero local supply, and the only way for tokens to appear on that chain is through a cross-chain send operation initiated from another chain in the mesh.

When a holder on Ethereum calls `send` with a destination chain ID corresponding to Arbitrum, the Ethereum contract burns the specified amount from the caller's balance, reducing Ethereum's local supply and the holder's local balance accordingly. A LayerZero message carrying the burn proof traverses to the Arbitrum contract, which validates the message's origin against its stored peer mapping, validates the DVN attestations against its security stack, and only then mints an equivalent amount of PYTHAI to the recipient address on Arbitrum. The recipient now holds tokens on Arbitrum that did not exist on Arbitrum a moment ago, but were not created from nothing — they were destroyed on Ethereum first. The sum of supplies across all chains is invariant.

The implication for the existence question is that the token's reality on a given chain is bounded by how much has been bridged in versus how much has been bridged out. Ethereum, having minted the original supply, can hold up to the full 111,111.111111111111111111 PYTHAI; Arbitrum can hold any amount that has been bridged in net of what has been bridged out, between zero and the global supply. The protocol does not require any minimum local supply for the contract to function — even a chain holding zero PYTHAI is still a PYTHAI chain in the sense that any user can receive a bridge transfer onto it, and the contract on that chain stands ready to mint into their balance the moment a valid LayerZero message arrives.

### Liquidity Topology Is Orthogonal to Token Existence

The question of where liquidity exists is entirely orthogonal to where the token exists. A chain can host the token contract and substantial holder balances without hosting any AMM pool, market maker, or trading venue. This is the case for a custody-heavy chain where users bridge tokens in to hold them long-term rather than to trade them. Conversely, a chain can have deep AMM pools without the chain itself being a primary holder of the token, because the pool's PYTHAI side comes from liquidity providers who bridged the tokens in specifically to deposit them as LP collateral.

The protocol supports three liquidity topologies, each of which is operationally valid and each of which produces a different user experience. The first topology is hub-and-spoke, where all meaningful liquidity is concentrated on Ethereum mainnet and the satellite chains host only the token contract with minimal or no AMM pools. Users on satellite chains can hold PYTHAI, receive PYTHAI through bridge transfers, and send PYTHAI to other addresses on their chain, but they cannot swap PYTHAI for USDC or any other asset on their local chain because no such pool exists. To trade, they must either call `send` to move their PYTHAI to Ethereum and swap on a Uniswap V3 pool there, or use the protocol's router pattern described below. This topology is acceptable when satellite-chain activity is dominated by holding and transferring rather than trading, and it minimizes the protocol's protocol-owned-liquidity footprint by concentrating market-making capital on the deepest venue.

The second topology is distributed, where every chain in the mesh hosts its own AMM pool with meaningful depth, allowing users on any chain to trade locally without bridging. This topology produces the best user experience because it minimizes friction for the median trader, but it requires the protocol to allocate protocol-owned liquidity across every chain, and it introduces the cross-chain price parity problem in its full form, because each pool is now an independent price-discovery venue that can disagree with every other.

The third topology is hybrid, where Ethereum hosts the dominant pool and one or two L2s host secondary pools with smaller but still meaningful depth, while remaining chains are pure custody-and-transfer venues. This is the most common end-state for OFT deployments in practice, because trading volume naturally concentrates where the deepest liquidity already exists, and the protocol's optimal liquidity allocation reflects that gravitational pull. BANKON PYTHAI launches in the hybrid configuration with Ethereum as the primary venue, Arbitrum and Base as secondary venues, and Optimism, Polygon, and Algorand as custody-and-transfer chains during the first year of operation, with the option to elevate any of the latter to secondary-venue status if local trading volume justifies the protocol-owned liquidity allocation.

### Centralized Liquidity Through Cross-Chain Routing

In the hub-and-spoke topology, where Ethereum holds essentially all liquidity, the price parity problem appears to vanish at first glance because there is only one price-discovery venue and it is whatever Ethereum's pool says it is at any given moment. This is true for the price-discovery question but not for the user-experience question, because users on satellite chains still need a way to express buy and sell intent without paying Ethereum gas to bridge for every trade.

The protocol addresses this through a router pattern. A `PythaiRouter` contract deployed on each satellite chain accepts USDC from a user, sends it via Circle's Cross-Chain Transfer Protocol to the Ethereum router, executes the swap on the Ethereum Uniswap V3 pool, and bridges the resulting PYTHAI back to the user on the originating satellite chain — all in a single user-facing transaction that hides the cross-chain choreography. The router charges a fee that covers the LayerZero and CCTP messaging costs plus a small protocol margin, and from the user's perspective the satellite chain has effectively borrowed Ethereum's liquidity for the duration of the trade.

This is functionally equivalent to having a local pool with infinite depth at the Ethereum price plus a routing fee, and it is appropriate when the satellite chain's trading volume does not justify the cost of maintaining a local pool. The break-even calculation is straightforward: a local pool of size L on the satellite chain incurs an opportunity cost equal to L multiplied by the yield the same capital could earn in Aave V3 on Ethereum, and that opportunity cost must be less than the routing fees the satellite chain's traders would otherwise pay. As the satellite chain's trading volume grows past the break-even point, the protocol allocates liquidity to a local pool, and the topology shifts from pure hub-and-spoke toward hybrid.

The critical insight is that even in the centralized-liquidity case, PYTHAI exists on every chain in the mesh as a first-class token, not as a wrapped representation. The router is a convenience layer, not a custodian, and users always retain the option to bridge manually through the raw OFT `send` function if they prefer to avoid the router or if the router is unavailable. The router itself holds no user funds beyond the duration of a single transaction, and there is no router-issued token that could trade at a discount to the underlying.

## Part Two: The Self-Sustaining Arbitrage Engine

### The Break-Even Equation for Cross-Chain Arbitrage

In any distributed or hybrid liquidity topology, the cross-chain price spread is bounded below by the round-trip cost of arbitrage. Letting `s` denote the per-token spread between two chains, `f_lz` the LayerZero message fee amortized over the trade size, `f_swap` the combined DEX fees on both sides, `f_gas` the combined gas costs on both chains amortized over the trade size, and `s_imp` the market impact slippage on both pools, an arbitrage is profitable when `s` exceeds the sum `f_lz + f_swap + f_gas + s_imp`. Below that threshold the spread is rational and persists until either an exogenous event widens it or the costs themselves fall.

The protocol's goal is not to drive `s` to zero, which would require subsidizing arbitrage at unbounded cost, but to drive `s` to the minimum value at which natural arbitrage is profitable, and to capture the residual value of the arbitrage opportunity for the treasury rather than allowing it to be paid entirely to external arbitrageurs. This is achieved by running the protocol's own arbitrage engine alongside the public market, using protocol-owned capital and the protocol's natural information advantage from operating the parity hub.

The break-even calculation also tells the protocol where its rebalancing efforts should focus. Chains where `f_gas` is high — Ethereum mainnet under congested conditions, for instance — will sustain wider equilibrium spreads, and the protocol should allocate proportionally more liquidity on those chains to reduce the `s_imp` slippage component and partially offset the irreducible gas cost. Chains where `f_gas` is essentially negligible, such as the current generation of L2s with sub-cent transaction costs, can sustain very tight equilibrium spreads, and the protocol's marginal liquidity allocation has correspondingly higher value on those chains.

### The Internal Arbitrage Bot

The `InternalArbiter` is a permissioned policy on the Bophades stack that holds operating capital in USDC, USDS, and PYTHAI on every chain in the mesh, and executes arbitrage trades whenever the parity hub reports a spread exceeding a configured minimum profitable threshold. The threshold is set above the pure break-even point to ensure that every executed trade produces positive net profit after costs, even accounting for execution risk and the time value of capital tied up in cross-chain settlement.

Concretely, when the hub reports that PYTHAI trades at $0.985 on Optimism and $1.005 on Arbitrum — a two-percent spread — the InternalArbiter computes the expected profit on a trade of size N as the spread times N minus the sum of all costs. If the result exceeds the minimum threshold, the bot executes. The Optimism leg buys PYTHAI from the Velodrome or Uniswap V4 pool using the bot's local USDC inventory, then calls `send` on the PYTHAI contract to bridge the acquired tokens to Arbitrum. On Arbitrum the bot's recipient address receives the minted PYTHAI through the OFT path and immediately sells into the Arbitrum pool for USDC, restoring the USDC inventory on the Arbitrum side. The net result is that Optimism's PYTHAI inventory increased and its USDC decreased, Arbitrum's PYTHAI decreased and its USDC increased, and the bot booked a profit equal to the spread minus costs.

The inventory imbalance produced by each round of arbitrage is the operational signal that tells the bot when to rebalance, and rebalancing is the most expensive class of action because it moves capital across chains without capturing a spread. The InternalArbiter therefore aims to alternate between profitable arbitrages in opposite directions, such that the net inventory drift across many trades is small. Practically, this works because price spreads on liquid pairs are roughly mean-reverting on short timescales — a chain that prices PYTHAI cheap today is no more likely to price it cheap again tomorrow than expensive — and the bot's profit and loss accumulates from the spread captures while the inventory wanders in a bounded range.

When the inventory does drift past a threshold on any chain, the bot triggers a rebalance, which uses Circle's CCTP to move USDC across chains at low cost, or in larger size uses an Aave V3 flash loan as described below. The rebalance trades are accounted separately from the arbitrage trades in the bot's reporting, so that the protocol can distinguish between profits earned from spread capture and the rebalancing overhead required to maintain operational position.

### Aave V3 Flash Loan Integration

A flash loan from Aave V3 turns the arbitrage from a capital-constrained activity into an opportunity-constrained activity. Without flash loans, the bot's per-chain inventory caps the maximum trade size it can execute, which means many profitable opportunities are foregone because the bot lacks USDC on the buying chain at the moment of opportunity. With flash loans, the bot borrows the required USDC from the Aave V3 Pool on the Ethereum hub, routes that USDC to the buying chain through CCTP within the flash loan callback, executes the full arbitrage cycle, returns the USDC to Aave with the nine-basis-point flash loan fee, and pockets the residual spread.

The full execution path within a single flash loan callback runs as follows. The bot invokes `pool.flashLoanSimple(receiver, USDC, amount, params, 0)` on Aave V3 Ethereum at `0x87870Bca3F3fD6335C3F4ce8392D69350B4fA4E2`, which transfers the requested USDC to the receiver and calls back into the receiver's `executeOperation` function. Inside that callback, the receiver burns the USDC to CCTP for delivery on the buying chain, which is sub-second-finality on Optimism and Arbitrum today. The bot then triggers the buy on the destination chain's pool, calls `send` to bridge the acquired PYTHAI to the selling chain, awaits the LayerZero message delivery, sells the PYTHAI for USDC on the selling chain, burns that USDC to CCTP back to Ethereum, and inside the still-open flash loan callback approves the Aave Pool to pull back the borrowed amount plus the flash fee.

The subtlety is that the LayerZero message delivery is not synchronous within a single Ethereum block, so the textbook flash loan structure has to be modified to accommodate the cross-chain delay. The protocol's implementation uses a deferred-settlement variant: the flash loan callback executes the Ethereum-side operations and emits a proof of the in-flight cross-chain leg, the Aave loan is repaid from a treasury reserve that the bot temporarily borrows against, and once the cross-chain settlement completes on the destination chain and the proceeds return through CCTP, the treasury reserve is replenished and the per-trade profit is realized. This costs a small additional risk premium for the duration of the cross-chain settlement, but it expands the available trade size from the bot's standing inventory to the much larger Aave V3 USDC liquidity, which currently stands well above one billion dollars on Ethereum mainnet.

For arbitrages small enough to complete within standing inventory, the flash loan path is bypassed and the bot trades from its own balance to avoid the nine-basis-point fee, which can otherwise consume a significant fraction of the spread on tighter opportunities. The threshold above which the flash loan path becomes economical is set as a function of the trade size and the spread; for typical opportunities on mid-sized L2 pools, the breakeven is around fifty thousand dollars of trade notional.

### The Self-Sustaining Economics

The arbitrage engine is self-sustaining when the profits it captures exceed the costs of operating it, where costs include gas, LayerZero fees, DEX fees, the Aave flash loan fee on the trades that use it, and the opportunity cost of the standing inventory deployed across chains. The opportunity cost is the dominant non-execution expense in steady state, because the bot must hold significant USDC and PYTHAI balances on every active chain to seed each side of an arbitrage, and that capital could otherwise earn yield in Aave V3 supply positions.

The protocol mitigates the opportunity cost by holding the standing inventory itself in Aave V3 positions whenever it is not actively deployed in an arbitrage. On Ethereum the USDC inventory sits in aEthUSDC at `0x98C23E9d8f34FEFb1B7BD6a91B7FF122F4e16F5c`, earning the prevailing supply rate. On Arbitrum and Base the inventory sits in the local Aave V3 deployment's aUSDC, earning the local rate. When the bot needs to execute, it withdraws from the aToken position in the same transaction as the swap, executes the arbitrage, and re-supplies the resulting USDC back to Aave before the transaction completes. The aToken withdraw-and-resupply cycle is gas-cheap relative to the arbitrage profit, and the net effect is that the standing inventory earns yield continuously except for the few seconds during each arbitrage execution.

The yield earned on the idle inventory directly offsets the opportunity cost, and on current Aave V3 rates of five to seven percent annualized on USDC, the offset substantially exceeds the bot's gas and fee expenses even at moderate trading frequency. The bot is therefore profitable in steady state, with profit flowing into the treasury and increasing the protocol's reserves over time.

A second self-reinforcing dynamic is that the protocol's natural information advantage compounds. The parity hub aggregates per-chain prices as they update on every swap, and the InternalArbiter consumes this data with the lowest possible latency because both contracts sit on the same chain and the hub's view functions can be read in the same block as the price update arrives. External arbitrageurs, by contrast, must subscribe to multiple chain RPCs and aggregate prices off-chain, introducing a latency of seconds at best. This gives the protocol's bot a first-mover advantage on the most profitable opportunities, and as a result the protocol captures a disproportionate share of the available arbitrage value, even when external arbitrageurs are active on the same pairs.

### The Marginal Profit Discipline

The arbitrage engine is constrained to take only marginally profitable trades by design, not by accident. The minimum profitability threshold is set as a function of the trade size and the cost basis, and the parameter is governed through the standard timelock so that it can be adjusted as gas costs and DEX fees evolve, but never to a value that would allow the bot to operate at a loss. Specifically, the threshold requires that the expected profit on a trade, after all costs and including a risk-adjusted reserve for execution slippage, exceeds a minimum of fifteen basis points of the trade notional. Trades below this threshold are passed over and left to external arbitrageurs.

This discipline ensures that the bot does not compete in the unprofitable tail of the opportunity distribution, where execution failures and adverse slippage events would consume the small expected profits and leave the protocol net negative. Instead, the bot captures the meat of the distribution — the spreads wide enough to be reliably profitable — and accepts that some opportunities will be taken by external actors instead.

A secondary benefit of the marginal-profit discipline is that it leaves room for external arbitrageurs to participate, which the protocol explicitly wants because external arbitrage tightens spreads further than the protocol's own bot would on its own. The protocol's bot acts as a backstop and a price-of-last-resort, taking the trades that external arbitrageurs find too small to be worth their attention but that still produce positive expected value when executed by a permissioned policy with lower gas overhead and zero front-running risk. The combined effect of internal and external arbitrage is to drive equilibrium spreads to a level approximately equal to the cost basis of the fastest external arbitrageur, which is typically tighter than what the protocol's bot would achieve on its own.

### The Rebate Mechanism for External Arbitrageurs

The protocol's bot operates alongside, not against, external arbitrageurs, and the rebate mechanism subsidizes external arbitrage during the gap-opening phase before the protocol's own bot reacts. The Dutch-auction rebate starts at zero immediately after a spread opens and ramps up linearly, so that the fastest external arbitrageurs capture the gap at zero subsidy, slower arbitrageurs capture it at small positive subsidy, and the protocol's bot acts as the final guarantor at the maximum subsidy if no external party has acted within the auction window. This structure pays the minimum necessary to incentivize convergence while maintaining the protocol's role as the backstop.

The rebate is paid from the treasury's emission budget, not from the arbitrage profits, and the protocol tracks the rebate-paid-versus-arbitrage-earned ratio as a key performance metric. In steady state this ratio should be substantially less than one, indicating that the protocol earns more from its own arbitrage than it pays out in rebates to external parties. If the ratio inverts and remains inverted for an extended period, governance can adjust either the rebate ramp parameters or the InternalArbiter's profitability threshold to bring the system back into a net-positive configuration.

### Aave GHO as an Optional Secondary Capital Source

For arbitrages that require capital larger than what flash loans can practically support — for example, a sustained position taken to close a multi-percent spread that requires a half-billion-dollar trade — the protocol can draw on its Aave V3 GHO borrow line. The treasury maintains a permissioned position that supplies wstETH and WBTC as collateral and borrows GHO against it, with the borrow capacity sized at a conservative loan-to-value ratio to avoid liquidation risk. The borrowed GHO is then either deposited into the sGHO savings vault at the current 4.25% APR when not in use, or routed into the arbitrage engine when a large opportunity opens.

The cost of capital from this path is the GHO borrow rate, which is governance-set with a hard cap at 9.5% APR per AIP-381, minus the sGHO yield when the GHO is parked. The net cost of standing capacity is well below one percent annualized in current market conditions, and the marginal cost of deploying it for an arbitrage is essentially zero because the position is already open. This gives the protocol a deep secondary reserve for opportunistic deployment without requiring it to hold large stablecoin balances directly, and the carry on the position is positive whenever sGHO yields more than the GHO borrow rate, which has been the case throughout sGHO's operating history.

The risk of the GHO borrow line is liquidation if the collateral value falls below the borrow value plus the liquidation buffer. The protocol manages this by maintaining a loan-to-value ratio substantially below the Aave liquidation threshold, by holding excess collateral that can be deployed to the position in adverse markets, and by integrating the position's health factor into the War Council emergency monitoring dashboard so that any deterioration triggers immediate review.

## Summary

BANKON PYTHAI exists on every chain where its contract is deployed, independently of whether any AMM pool exists on that chain, because the contract is a real ERC20 with real balances recorded in the chain's state, not a wrapped or synthetic representation. The supply across chains is held invariant by the LayerZero OFT burn-and-mint mechanism, and the topology of where liquidity is concentrated is a separate operational question that can range from full concentration on Ethereum to full distribution across every chain, with the protocol supporting any configuration through a combination of native AMM pools and the satellite-chain router pattern.

The arbitrage engine is the mechanism that enforces price parity in the distributed and hybrid topologies, and it is structured to be self-sustaining through the combination of natural information advantage at the parity hub, idle-inventory yield earned in Aave V3 supply positions, optional flash-loan capital amplification from the Aave V3 Pool, and a marginal-profit discipline that ensures the bot only executes when the expected return exceeds all costs by a meaningful margin. The protocol pays rebates to external arbitrageurs through a Dutch-auction mechanism that activates only when external speed is needed and degrades gracefully into protocol-internal execution otherwise. The economic result, in steady state, is a price spread bounded above by the round-trip cost of arbitrage, a treasury that accumulates profit from the spreads its bot captures, and a permissioned policy that operates within timelock-gated parameters subject to governance review.

The system's most important property is that it does not require trust in any single chain, any single oracle, or any single market maker. Each chain's price is measured directly from the AMM pool on that chain, the aggregation is performed by a contract whose source is public and whose logic is governance-controlled, and the arbitrage is executed by a bot whose every action is recorded on-chain and auditable. The protocol's treasury holds the capital, the protocol's policies execute the trades, and the protocol's governance sets the parameters, all under the same Bophades framework that governs the rest of the IDC v3 stack.
