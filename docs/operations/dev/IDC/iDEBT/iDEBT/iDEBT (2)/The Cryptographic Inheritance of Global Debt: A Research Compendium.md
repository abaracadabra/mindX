### 1.4 Structural unsustainability: the academic case

**Reinhart and Rogoff** established the dominant framework with "Growth in a Time of Debt" (*American Economic Review*, 2010; NBER WP 15639) and *This Time Is Different* (Princeton, 2009). Their headline claim: above **90% debt-to-GDP, median growth falls by 1 percentage point**, and average growth falls considerably more. Their follow-up paper "Debt Overhangs: Past and Present" (NBER WP 18015, 2012) found that in 23 of 26 historical episodes where debt exceeded 90%, growth was lower, with an average reduction of 1.2% lasting an average of **23 years**. Critically, "in 11 of the 26 episodes, real interest rates were the same or lower than in other periods — yet growth was similarly impaired."

The Herndon, Ash, and Pollin critique ("Does High Public Debt Consistently Stifle Economic Growth?", *Cambridge Journal of Economics*, 2014) discovered a coding error and methodological issues, finding that the corrected result showed **2.2% real annual growth above 90% debt-to-GDP, not −0.1%**. A 2022 meta-analysis found no robust threshold effect after controlling for publication bias. The current consensus: the 90% threshold lacks universal support, but debt overhangs do empirically correlate with slower growth through multiple channels.

- **Full citation:** Reinhart, Carmen M., and Kenneth S. Rogoff, "Growth in a Time of Debt," *American Economic Review* 100(2): 573–578, 2010. DOI: 10.3386/w15639.
- **Full citation:** Herndon, Thomas, Michael Ash, and Robert Pollin, "Does High Public Debt Consistently Stifle Economic Growth? A Critique of Reinhart and Rogoff," *Cambridge Journal of Economics* 38(2): 257–279, 2014. DOI: 10.1093/cje/bet075.
- **Full citation:** Reinhart, C.M., V.R. Reinhart, and K.S. Rogoff, "Debt Overhangs: Past and Present," NBER WP 18015, 2012. https://www.nber.org/papers/w18015

**Olivier Blanchard's** AEA Presidential Address ("Public Debt and Low Interest Rates," *AER* 109(4): 1197–1229, 2019) argued the opposite: when the safe interest rate remains below the growth rate (r < g), "debt rollovers may well be feasible. Put bluntly, public debt may have no fiscal cost." He showed this condition is "more the historical norm than the exception." His 2023 book *Fiscal Policy under Low Interest Rates* (MIT Press) updated the argument post-rate hikes, maintaining that "r-g likely to remain negative on average, with high probability" while acknowledging the critical caveat that "as d [debt] increases, r* and by implication r will increase" — endogeneity of interest rates to debt levels that could undermine the r < g condition.

**Ray Dalio's** *Principles for Navigating Big Debt Crises* (2018) and *How Countries Go Broke* (2025) present the **Long-Term Debt Cycle** framework: five phases from sound money through debt bubble, pop, deleveraging, to new equilibrium. His key insight for the thesis: four levers of deleveraging (austerity, defaults, money printing, wealth transfers), with "beautiful deleveraging" requiring a balanced mix of deflationary restructuring and inflationary monetization. Dalio stated in 2018: "It appears to me that we are in the late stages of both the short-term and long-term debt cycles." His three rules: don't let debt rise faster than income; don't let income rise faster than productivity; do everything to raise productivity.

**Carmen Reinhart and M. Belen Sbrancia** ("The Liquidation of Government Debt," IMF WP 15/007; NBER WP 16893) documented how governments historically reduce debt through **financial repression** — "directed lending to government by captive domestic audiences, explicit or implicit caps on interest rates, regulation of cross-border capital movements." Real interest rates were negative roughly **half the time during 1945–1980**, liquidating debt at **3–4% of GDP annually** in the US and UK, ~5% in Australia and Italy. This is the historical playbook for dealing with debt overhangs of this magnitude.

### 1.5 Fiscal dominance and doom loops

**Sargent and Wallace** ("Some Unpleasant Monetarist Arithmetic," *Fed Reserve Bank of Minneapolis Quarterly Review*, 1981) proved that in a fiscally dominant regime, "a central bank may not have the power to determine the long-run rate of inflation without fiscal support" — tighter money today can cause **higher inflation in the future** if fiscal deficits are predetermined. Andolfatto (2021, Fed St. Louis) reviewed this 40 years later, warning that the coexistence of low inflation, low rates, and high deficits "may be fragile."

The **sovereign-bank doom loop** was formalized by Brunnermeier et al. ("The Sovereign-Bank Diabolic Loop and ESBies," *AER* 106(5): 508–512, 2016; NBER WP 21993) and Farhi and Tirole ("Deadly Embrace," *Review of Economic Studies* 85(3): 1781–1823, 2018). Banks hold domestic sovereign debt → sovereign stress reduces bank asset values → weakened banks require bailouts → sovereign risk increases → feedback loop. Garicano (2025) warns: "Italian banks remain exposed to Italian risk, Spanish banks to Spanish risk. The next crisis will find Europe where it was in 2012: banks and sovereigns locked in mutual destruction."

### 1.6 MMT counterarguments

**Stephanie Kelton** (*The Deficit Myth*, 2020) articulates the Modern Monetary Theory position: sovereign currency issuers cannot go bankrupt in their own currency; **inflation, not solvency, is the real constraint**; deficits become problematic only when spending exceeds real productive capacity. The academic establishment strongly contests this: a Chicago Booth IGM Forum survey found **not a single respondent** among 50 leading economists agreed with MMT's central claims. Paul Krugman called MMT principles "indefensible"; Kenneth Rogoff called it "nonsense"; Sebastian Edwards (*Cato Journal*/Hoover) documented Latin American cases where MMT-like policies produced hyperinflation. The thesis should present MMT as the strongest counterargument to the unsustainability claim while noting its marginal academic status.

## Part I supplement — Refinancing risk and the maturity wall

### 1.7 Sovereign and corporate refinancing schedules

The **OECD Global Debt Report 2025** (https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/global-debt-report-2025_8ee42b13-en.html) reports OECD sovereign bond issuance hit a record **$17 trillion in 2025**, projected at **~$18 trillion in 2026**. Refinancing requirements: record **~$13.5 trillion in 2025**, accounting for ~80% of gross borrowing. **Almost 45% of OECD sovereign debt will mature by 2027**, including one-third of fixed-rate debt — 60% of which was issued before the post-2022 tightening cycle at significantly lower rates. Government interest payments increased to **3.3% of GDP** across the OECD in 2024, now exceeding aggregate defense spending. Central bank holdings of domestic sovereigns fell from **29% (2021) to 19% (2024)**, shifting refinancing burden to price-sensitive private investors.

For the US specifically, **~$9.2 trillion** in marketable Treasury debt matured in 2025, with a further ~$9 trillion maturing in 2026 (Deloitte Insights, September 2025). Corporate debt maturities jump from ~$2 trillion (2024) to **~$3 trillion in 2026** (S&P Global). Roughly **one-third of all outstanding corporate bond debt will mature by 2027** (OECD), and the weighted cost of that maturing debt is lower than current market yields, meaning interest expenditure will increase on refinancing. The OECD notes: "Rather than productive investment, a lot of debt in recent years has been used to fund financial operations like refinancings and shareholder payouts."

Emerging markets face record **$9 trillion in debt redemptions in 2026** (IIF), with low-income, high-risk countries having **over half their debt maturing within three years**.

### 1.8 BIS debt service ratios

The **BIS debt service ratio** dataset (https://www.bis.org/statistics/dsr.htm) covers 32 countries. The BIS characterizes the aggregate DSR as "an accurate early warning indicator of systemic banking crises at horizons of up to one to two years in advance" (Drehmann et al., 2017). The BIS CGFS Paper No. 67, "Private sector debt and financial stability," flags that credit-to-GDP gaps prior to COVID were higher than those preceding the Global Financial Crisis in ~half of sampled economies — rising to ~60% by 2021. Falling interest rates had contained debt servicing costs for a decade, but this dynamic has now reversed.

The **IMF Fiscal Monitor (October 2025)** warned: "The years between the global financial crisis and the pandemic were marked by unusually easy conditions for sustaining debt. Rising debt was accompanied by falling interest rates, leading to an overall stable interest bill on budget. But the situation is now starkly different."

## Part III — The recursive proof: oracle design and technical architecture

### 3.1 Perpetual futures: academic foundations

The definitive academic treatment is **Ackerer, Hugonnier, and Jermann** ("Perpetual Futures Pricing," *Mathematical Finance*, 2025; NBER WP 32936; arXiv:2310.11771). They derive "explicit expressions for the no-arbitrage price of various perpetual contracts, including linear, inverse, and quantos futures." The paper establishes that "the futures price is given by the risk-neutral expectation of the spot sampled at a random time that reflects the intensity of the price anchoring." Historically, perpetual futures were "formally introduced by Shiller (1993)" but popularized by BitMEX in 2016. As of H1 2023, perpetuals account for **75% of the $27 billion daily average volume** and **94% of the $8 billion daily average open interest** in crypto derivatives.

### 3.2 Protocol architectures compared

Three dominant designs exist. **GMX** uses a unified multi-asset liquidity pool (no order book), with borrowing fees rather than traditional funding rates and Chainlink oracle prices for "zero price impact trades." **dYdX v4** runs as a standalone Cosmos SDK app-chain with a validator-maintained off-chain orderbook, CometBFT consensus, and traditional funding rate mechanics. **Hyperliquid** operates a custom L1 with HyperBFT consensus and a fully on-chain central limit order book (CLOB), claiming 10,000+ orders/second with sub-second latency and holding **over 75% market share** in perpetual DEX trading as of 2025.

### 3.3 Oracle architectures: push, pull, and composable

**Chainlink** uses a three-layer push model: data source aggregation → independent node operator aggregation → on-chain FluxAggregator/OCR taking the median. Updates trigger by deviation threshold (0.5–1%) or heartbeat timeout. **Pyth Network** pioneered the pull oracle model: first-party data providers publish to Pythnet (Solana VM appchain) → aggregation every **400 milliseconds** → Wormhole-signed VAAs → users pull prices on-demand to target chains. Pyth claims **5–10× reduction in price tracking error** vs. competing oracles, sourcing from 100+ institutional data providers (Jane Street, LMAX, Cboe, Binance). **Synthetix V3** implements an **Oracle Manager** — a composable, oracle-agnostic system allowing market creators to "choose from multiple oracle solutions to set up custom aggregation... Example: Use the lowest price for spot Bitcoin based on Chainlink, Pyth, and Uniswap's time-weighted average price (TWAP)."

- **Full citation (Pyth):** Pyth Network Blog, "Pull, Don't Push." https://www.pyth.network/blog/pyth-a-new-model-to-the-price-oracle
- **Full citation (Synthetix):** Synthetix V3 Blog. https://blog.synthetix.io/what-is-synthetix-v3/

Oracle manipulation is the **most frequent incident type in DeFi** (MDPI *Entropy* 25(1):60, 2023). Defenses include median aggregation, TWAP with truncation (Uniswap V4 caps maximum price movement per block at 9116 ticks), multi-source composability, and circuit breakers. The academic frontier includes OVer (ICSE 2024), the first sound verification tool for oracle deviation, and SecPLF (ASIACCS 2024), which formalizes adversary models for lending protocols.

### 3.4 Insurance fund design and the ADL trilemma

An arXiv paper ("Autodeleveraging: Impossibilities and Optimization," 2512.01112v2) proves a **fundamental trilemma**: "No policy can simultaneously satisfy exchange solvency, revenue, and fairness to traders. This impossibility theorem implies that as participation scales, a novel form of moral hazard grows asymptotically, rendering 'zero-loss' socialization impossible." Production protocols implement tiered loss management: liquidation penalties → insurance fund → auto-deleveraging (last resort). The October 2025 market crash saw **$5 billion in liquidations** overwhelm insurance funds, with Hyperliquid triggering cross-margin ADL for the first time in over two years.

### 3.5 Liquidation mechanics

**Qin et al.** ("An Empirical Study of DeFi Liquidations," ACM *Internet Measurement Conference*, 2021; arXiv:2106.06389) provided the first comprehensive study across Aave, Compound, MakerDAO, and dYdX (85%+ of Ethereum lending), finding "existing liquidation designs well incentivize liquidators but sell excessive amounts of discounted collateral at the borrowers' expenses." Sadeghi et al. (arXiv:2602.12104, 2025) introduced the concept of **Oracle Extractable Value (OEV)**, where liquidators manipulate AMMs with sandwich attacks to trigger profitable liquidations, proving that "CPMM transaction fees act as a critical security parameter."

## Historical precedent: paradigm displacement in monetary systems

### From gold to fiat: the Bretton Woods collapse

**Bordo** ("The Operation and Demise of the Bretton Woods System: 1958 to 1971," NBER WP 23189, 2017) documents how the system collapsed through inflationary US monetary policy, loss of confidence, and Nixon's August 15, 1971 suspension of gold convertibility — "an event on par with the ending of the classical gold standard at the outbreak of WWI in August 1914." **Butkiewicz and Ohlmacher** (*Economic History Review* 74: 922–945, 2021), analyzing Nixon's actual tape recordings, found that "Nixon resisted ending the BWS for political reasons and did not fully understand the NEP's strategy." The lesson: a monetary system that seemed permanent was abandoned in a weekend. The transition from gold-backed to fiat happened through crisis and unilateral action, not gradual reform.

### Parallel systems escaping sovereign control

The **Eurodollar market** emerged "more or less by accident" in 1956 (Kindleberger, 1970), when London bankers created USD-denominated credit instruments outside US regulation. Murau (*Journal of Institutional Economics*, 2020) documents how a 1972–73 "Battle of Basel" failed to regulate the market, which then became the mechanism for petrodollar recycling and grew beyond any authority's capacity to contain. By 2008, the Fed had to establish emergency swap lines with 14 central banks to stabilize the offshore dollar system.

**Shadow banking** reached over **$67 trillion** by 2011 (FSB) — nearly half of global bank assets.
